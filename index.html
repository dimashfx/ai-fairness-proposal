Generative AI Use and Perceptions of Academic Fairness Among Undergraduate Students at Nazarbayev University

A positivist research proposal for Fall 2026

Name

Dinmukhamed Marat

Course

PLS210 Political Science Research Methods

Many students now use generative AI (GenAI) tools such as ChatGPT for coursework. This proposal asks whether students who use GenAI more often see AI-assisted academic work as more acceptable and more fair. It plans to test this idea with survey data from undergraduate students at Nazarbayev University (NU) in Fall 2026.

2

Research question

How does the use of generative AI affect undergraduate students’ perceptions of academic fairness at Nazarbayev University in Fall 2026?

Population

Undergraduate students enrolled at Nazarbayev University.

Context

Use of GenAI tools (for example ChatGPT) for academic work, such as assignments, studying and assessment.

Location

Nazarbayev University, Astana, Kazakhstan.

Time period

Fall 2026 semester.

Why the question is empirical and answerable

The question is empirical because evidence from the real world can answer it. It does not ask whether students should use AI. It asks whether the way students use AI is related to what they think is fair. Both parts can be measured:

  • GenAI use can be measured with survey questions about how often, and for which tasks, students use it.
  • Fairness perceptions can be measured with rating-scale statements, for example how fair a student thinks it is to use AI to edit an essay.
  • The population and the time period are clear, so data can be collected from a sample of NU undergraduates in one semester.
  • The hypothesis can be supported or not supported by the data.

One limit: the word “affect” suggests cause and effect. A single survey can show that two things are related, but it cannot fully prove that one causes the other. I return to this point in the reflection.

3

Epistemological positioning

This study is positivist.

What positivism means

Positivism is a way of thinking about knowledge. It says we can learn about the social world in a similar way to how natural scientists learn about nature: by observing it carefully, measuring it, and testing ideas against evidence. Its main ideas are:

  • The social world can be studied through observable facts, and these facts exist independently of the researcher.
  • Ideas are turned into variables that can be measured, for example with a survey scale.
  • The researcher tries to stay neutral and uses the same procedure with every participant.
  • Knowledge grows by testing hypotheses. A hypothesis is a clear prediction that the data can support or fail to support.
  • The goal is to find patterns that hold beyond the people in one sample.

Why positivism fits this research question

  • The question links two variables, GenAI use and fairness perceptions. Positivist research is designed to test relationships between variables.
  • Both variables can be measured with standard survey questions. Perceptions are treated as attitudes that students rate on a scale.
  • The population is large. A survey with statistical analysis lets me check whether a pattern in a sample is likely to exist in the wider population.
  • My hypothesis (H1) is a clear prediction with a direction, so it can be tested.
  • Similar studies used survey designs (Chan, 2025; Farinosi & Melchior, 2025; Zhunusbekova & Askarkyzy, 2025), so my results could be compared with theirs.

The trade-off: positivism does not capture how students explain their views in their own words. An interpretivist study, such as the focus groups of O’Dea et al. (2025), answers a different question. This is a trade-off, not a mistake.

4

Contribution

What existing research already tells us

In the six sources reviewed in the next section, students use GenAI widely. In several studies they disapprove of using AI to produce a whole assignment, but they are less sure about smaller uses. Many find the boundaries unclear and ask for clearer rules. When one study asked directly whether AI use is fair, the most common answer was “it depends”.

What is missing

Most of this work describes students’ views. Few studies test whether students who use GenAI more often judge AI-related practices differently. Fairness itself is rarely the main focus. Evidence from Kazakhstan is limited, and none of the six sources looks at NU in Fall 2026.

What this study contributes

  • Empirical: a test of the link between how often students use GenAI and how fair they think AI-related academic practices are, using NU data from Fall 2026.
  • Conceptual: a clear separation of the IV (use) from the DV (fairness perceptions), and a proposed mechanism that could explain the link.
  • Practical: if H1 is supported, universities would learn that light and heavy users may hold different views of fairness, which could help them design clearer AI guidance.

Student use and views of GenAI

Surveys in Europe show that most students already know GenAI tools. In a survey carried out to inform the University of Liverpool’s academic integrity code, only 7% of respondents had not heard of any GenAI technology, and more than half had used or considered using GenAI for academic purposes (Johnston et al., 2024). In Italy, Farinosi and Melchior (2025) found that 38.7% of students said they used GenAI for assignments or exams when asked directly, but 48.8% reported academic use when asked about specific ways of using it. The authors suggest that social desirability may lead students to under-report academic use. This matters for my study, because my IV depends on honest self-reports.

Academic integrity and changing ideas of misconduct

Students do not treat all AI use in the same way. Johnston et al. (2024) report that 54.1% of students were supportive or somewhat supportive of tools such as Grammarly, while 70.4% were unsupportive or somewhat unsupportive of using ChatGPT to write a whole essay. Chan (2025) asked students about different scenarios and found clear disapproval of direct AI content generation but ambivalent attitudes to subtler uses. She argues that this challenges traditional definitions of academic misconduct, which need to adapt. In Italy, 58.3% of students agreed, strongly or slightly, that using ChatGPT for tasks and exams is morally wrong (Farinosi & Melchior, 2025). In Kazakhstan, 57% of students saw no significant conflict between AI use and academic integrity (Zhunusbekova & Askarkyzy, 2025). The questions differ, so these two figures cannot be compared directly, but they suggest that views may vary by context.

Ethical and equitable use

O’Dea et al. (2025) held 20 focus groups with 80 students at four UK institutions. Students were keen to use GenAI to support learning, but they had significant concerns about where good practice ends and where they could be accused of cheating, or could limit their own learning. The authors report that unclear institutional guidance appeared to cause confusion about what counts as ethical use. On equity, some students said free versions make GenAI available to everyone, while others said that paid versions are only available to those who can afford them, which could make use unequal in assessment.

Fairness perceptions

Delaney et al. (2025) asked 36 undergraduates at one large US university, “Do you think using AI on school assignments is fair?” Most did not give a simple yes or no. Fifteen said it depends on the situation, six said it is fair, five said it is not fair, and eight gave more than one kind of answer. Their reasons included the purpose of the AI use, the course instructions, and the capabilities of free chatbots. The authors suggest that, without consistent policies, students relied on their own beliefs, for example whether AI helped them learn. They also say their sample is small and not meant to be generalised, and they call for larger surveys with validated measures.

The Kazakhstan context

Zhunusbekova and Askarkyzy (2025) surveyed 840 undergraduates at three Kazakhstani universities in May 2024. According to the abstract, 90% were familiar with ChatGPT and 65% used AI tools at least weekly for academic purposes. The main uses were essay writing (35%), problem solving (25%) and idea generation (18%). Also, 96% wanted clear institutional policies. This is the only Kazakhstan source in this review. Its abstract reports descriptive percentages and does not name the three universities.

Research gap

The six sources agree on three things: students use GenAI widely, their judgments depend on how AI is used, and they want clearer rules. Four things are still missing:

  1. Use and views are rarely linked. Most studies describe views. Farinosi and Melchior compare views mainly by gender, field of study and digital skills. As far as I can tell from these six sources, none tests how often students use GenAI for academic work as an explanation of their fairness views.
  2. Fairness is rarely the main focus. Only Delaney et al. ask about fairness directly, in a small interview study at one US university.
  3. Local evidence is thin. The one Kazakhstan survey is descriptive and from 2024. None of the six sources studies NU in Fall 2026.
  4. Use is changing fast. O’Dea et al. report UK survey figures (from Freeman, 2024, 2025) in which the share of undergraduates using GenAI for assessment rose from 53% to 88% in one year. Older data may not describe students today.

This gap leads to my research question: is the way students use GenAI related to how fair they think AI-related academic practices are, at NU in Fall 2026?

Theory and Hypotheses

  • IV: independent variable
    Frequency / intensity of GenAI use
    How often, and how heavily, a student uses GenAI for academic work

    Mechanism
    Changing understandings of legitimate academic work
    What a student thinks counts as acceptable, “real” student work

    DV: dependent variable
    Perceptions of academic fairness
    Whether AI-assisted work, assessment and levels of AI use are seen as fair

Figure 1. The proposed path. H1 predicts a positive association: more GenAI use goes together with more permissive fairness perceptions.

The independent variable (IV)

Frequency / intensity of GenAI use for academic work. This is how often a student uses tools such as ChatGPT for coursework (for example, days per week), and how heavily (for example, using AI only to check grammar, or to draft whole sections). It would be measured with self-report survey items.

The dependent variable (DV)

Perceptions of academic fairness. This is how fair students think AI-related academic practices are. It has three parts: (1) whether AI-assisted academic work is fair, (2) whether assessment is fair when AI is available, and (3) whether different levels of AI use are fair, from grammar checking to writing a whole essay. A more permissive perception means a student judges more of these practices as acceptable and fair.

The mechanism

Changing understandings of legitimate academic work. This means a student’s beliefs about what counts as real, acceptable student work. A student who has never used AI may think that any AI help is cheating. A student who uses it often may come to see it as a normal tool, like a spell checker.

Why the mechanism connects the IV and the DV

  1. Students who use GenAI more often see more AI-assisted work, both their own and their classmates’.
  2. This exposure can make AI help feel normal. It also teaches students what AI can and cannot do, so their idea of “my own work” may change.
  3. A changed idea of legitimate work changes their judgments of fairness. Practices that once looked like cheating may look acceptable.

This idea is consistent with two sources: Chan (2025) argues that AI challenges traditional definitions of misconduct, and Delaney et al. (2025) found that students used their own beliefs to judge fairness. However, neither study tests this mechanism. It is my theoretical proposal.

Hypothesis

H1: More frequent use of generative AI for academic work is associated with more permissive perceptions of AI-related academic practices and fairness.

H0 (null): There is no association between the frequency of GenAI use for academic work and perceptions of AI-related academic practices and fairness.

Other explanations to keep in mind

The direction of H1 is not certain. Heavy users could become more critical if they see AI’s mistakes and risks. O’Dea et al. (2025) report that, as students’ knowledge grew, they became more sceptical of GenAI. The link could also run the other way: students who already think AI use is fair may simply use it more. A third factor, such as field of study or attitude to technology, could influence both. A positivist design handles this by measuring such factors and checking them statistically.

What working with AI helped me understand, and what I still don’t understand about my research design.

This section is ungraded.

What AI helped me understand

  • The IV, the DV and the mechanism are different things. At first I treated the mechanism as just another variable. Now I see it as the explanation for why the IV should affect the DV. It is the “because” in my argument.
  • What positivism means for this project. When I saw that my question is about two things I can measure, the positivist choice made sense. I could also see what it leaves out: students’ own explanations.
  • How to write a literature review by themes. Grouping the six sources by idea showed me the gap much more clearly than summarising them one by one.
  • The same kind of number can mean different things. The studies ask different questions, so I should not compare their percentages directly.

What I still do not fully understand

  • Permissive versus fair. H1 talks about “permissive perceptions” and about “fairness”. I am not sure these are the same. A student might think AI use is allowed but still unfair, for example because some students can pay for better tools. I need to decide how to measure them separately.
  • Cause and effect. My question says “affect”, but one survey only shows association. Students who already think AI is fine might just use it more. I do not yet know the best way to deal with this with data I could realistically collect.
  • Measuring the mechanism. “Changing understandings” sounds like something that happens over time. I am not sure how to measure it in a single survey.
  • The NU context. I have not yet read NU’s own rules on AI. The literature suggests that university guidance shapes what students think, so this could matter a lot for my results.
